1. Project Overview

Introduction

A Wi-Fi network has a limited amount of available bandwidth. When multiple students connect to the same network, the available capacity must be distributed among them.

One simple approach is to divide the bandwidth equally. However, students may have different bandwidth demands. Some may need only a small amount, while others require more.

This project investigates how mathematical models can allocate bandwidth according to individual demand and priority weights while respecting the total network capacity.

Research Question

How can rational functions and proportional allocation be used to simulate the distribution of limited Wi-Fi bandwidth among students with changing demands?

Project Objectives

  • Model equal bandwidth sharing using rational functions.
  • Derive a weighted proportional allocation formula.
  • Implement demand-aware redistribution.
  • Simulate changing network conditions.
  • Compare adaptive allocation with rigid equal limits.
  • Visualize bandwidth utilization mathematically.

2. Mathematical Model

2.1 Equal Bandwidth Allocation

Suppose a network has a total bandwidth capacity of BT Mbps and n connected students.

If bandwidth is divided equally, each student receives the following share:

B(n) = BT / n

For a network with 100 Mbps:

B(n) = 100 / n

This is a rational function because the variable appears in the denominator.

As the number of students increases, the equal bandwidth share decreases.

The mathematical graph of B(n) = 100/n has a vertical asymptote at n = 0 and a horizontal asymptote at B = 0.

In the actual simulation, n is restricted to positive whole numbers, so n = 0 is excluded.

2.2 Weighted Allocation

Students may have different priority weights. A student with a larger weight receives a larger proportional share before demand limits are considered.

Bi = (wi / Σwj) × BT

Where:

  • Bi: allocated bandwidth for student i
  • wi: priority weight of student i
  • Σwj: sum of all active priority weights
  • BT: total available bandwidth

2.3 Derivation

Assume that each student's bandwidth allocation is proportional to their priority weight:

Bi = k × wi

If the entire capacity is distributed:

ΣBi = BT

Substituting the proportional relationship:

k × Σwi = BT

Therefore:

k = BT / Σwi

Substituting back:

Bi = (wi / Σwj) × BT

2.4 Demand-Aware Redistribution

Pure proportional allocation does not account for students who need less bandwidth than their calculated share.

The adaptive algorithm therefore:

  1. Calculates weighted shares among active students.
  2. Identifies students whose demands can be fully satisfied.
  3. Allocates only the bandwidth those students need.
  4. Removes satisfied students from the active allocation set.
  5. Redistributes the remaining capacity among unsatisfied students.
  6. Repeats until no further redistribution is possible.

2.5 Mathematical Constraints

0 ≤ Bi ≤ di
ΣBi ≤ BT

Here di represents student i's requested bandwidth.

These constraints ensure that no student receives negative bandwidth, no student receives more than requested, and total allocation does not exceed capacity.

6. Results and Conclusions

Basic Experimental Example

Consider a network with a total capacity of 100 Mbps and three students with different bandwidth demands.

Student Demand Fixed Equal Adaptive
1 10 Mbps 10 Mbps 10 Mbps
2 30 Mbps 30 Mbps 30 Mbps
3 80 Mbps 33.33 Mbps 60 Mbps

Utilization Comparison

73.33% Fixed equal utilization
100% Adaptive utilization
26.67 Percentage-point improvement

Interpretation

Under rigid equal limits, each student is initially limited to approximately 33.33 Mbps.

Students 1 and 2 cannot use their entire shares because their demands are lower. Student 3 cannot borrow the unused capacity.

The adaptive algorithm instead allocates 10 Mbps and 30 Mbps to the first two students, then redistributes the remaining 60 Mbps to Student 3.

In this example, the adaptive method increases allocated bandwidth utilization from approximately 73.33% to 100%.

Conclusion

This project demonstrates that rational functions and proportional relationships can form the foundation of a mathematical bandwidth allocation model.

The demand-aware extension allows unused capacity to be redistributed while respecting total capacity and individual demand constraints.

The simulation illustrates how mathematical modeling, algorithms, and programming can be combined to study resource allocation.

Limitations

  • The network demands are simulated rather than measured.
  • The model assumes a fixed total bandwidth capacity.
  • The model does not account for latency, packet loss, or wireless interference.
  • The simulation does not control real network hardware.
  • The comparison uses rigid equal limits, not every existing network scheduling method.
  • Higher modeled allocation utilization does not automatically prove higher real-world network performance.
